Background Noise

Teaching a model to recognize bird species from field audio, and learning that the exciting-looking result was a leak.

01 · The real bottleneck

Labeling, not recording

Field microphones run continuously and pull in hours of wind, rain, insects and silence, with the actual bird calls scattered somewhere inside. Recording is easy. The expensive part is labeling: someone has to tag clips by hand, and the model only learns from what's been tagged.

So most of my work went into the question underneath all of that: which clips are worth a human's time to label? That's the active learning loop. I built several strategies for choosing those clips (top-k entropy, dynamic batch sizing off an entropy threshold, and Query-by-Committee using Monte Carlo Dropout) plus a reinforcement-learning component that adjusts the labeling budget as training goes, instead of spending it at a flat rate.

02 · The model was cheating

It looked like recognition. It was memorization.

Early on the model looked like it was doing well. It wasn't. Clips from the same field recording were landing in both the training and test sets, so the model was quietly learning the background hiss of individual recordings, the specific hum of one microphone on one day, instead of the birds.

I rewrote the train/test split so it separates by recording, meaning no recording ever appears on both sides. Accuracy dropped, which was the point: the lower number was the honest one, and everything measured after that was finally measuring the thing we cared about.

03 · What the numbers say

Less exciting, more honest

On 25 species from the public BirdSet data, averaged over three seeds, margin sampling reached 85.5% accuracy with 348 labels, compared with 73.2% when clips were picked at random. Coreset, which picks clips to cover the data evenly, did worse than random, at 65.9%.

I also built a bandit agent that decides how many clips to label each round. An early comparison made it look like a big win, until I noticed it had been given more rounds than the fixed baseline. Once every method had the same label budget, the bandit matched a well-chosen fixed budget instead of beating it. That's a less exciting result, and it's the honest one.

04 · Where I sit on this

Field intuition and follow-through

I'm not the strongest coder in the room, and I don't pretend to be. What I bring is field intuition and follow-through. Before I opened a single spectrogram, I'd spent weeks in Belize snorkeling through mangrove roots and watching a bird migration pass overhead, so I already had a feel for why monitoring wildlife in a noisy real environment is so hard. When I joined, I read the lab's papers cold and used AI tools to fill the gaps in my background, so I could contribute early instead of spending a month catching up.

05 · What it turned into

A question about trust

What stuck with me was less about the accuracy score and more about trust. A biologist has to act on this model's output without being able to see inside it. If it says "probably species X, but not confident," what are they supposed to do with that? Sitting with that question is what turned me toward human-centered design.

Built with Jeremy Frank and Shrivas Sudharsan on public BirdSet data. My part is in the public repo linked above.
