The 30-Minute Lie

I set out to prove that UCSD's dining app gives wrong wait times. Then I pulled 125 real orders, mine and a friend's, and the data told a more interesting story.

Role
Solo: data collection, analysis, design
Data
125 Triton2Go food orders from two students (77 mine, 48 a friend's), May–Nov 2026, from emailed receipts
Tools
Python, Figma
Finding
Where the app gives an estimate, it pads it: 84 of 110 orders were ready early. The Bistro, the slowest spot, never gave an estimate at all: 11 orders, 10 to 41 minutes, median 30.

01 · The hunch

I thought the timer was lying

This started as me being annoyed, not as a design exercise. I'd order at the Bistro before a 1pm lecture and end up standing outside way past when I expected to eat. My theory: the app estimates every dish the same way, active tickets times an average prep time, and ignores that a wok station only has so many burners.

That theory made a nice story. Before designing around it, I wanted to check it.

02 · Testing it

125 receipts

Getting the data was its own obstacle. The app blocks screenshots (the screen goes white), and receipts can only be emailed to the account's own address. So every order sends an email receipt with the order time, the app's target time, and when the order was marked complete. I collected 77 of my food orders from May to November 2026. To check that this wasn't just my habits, a friend forwarded her receipts too: 48 more orders. Together, that's a dataset of 125 orders: location, dish, time ordered, time quoted, time actually completed.

The lie wasn't the number. It was the missing one.

At the places that give an estimate, the estimate is padded, not optimistic: 84 of 110 orders (76%) were ready early, typically 4 minutes before the quoted time, and my friend's orders showed the same pattern as mine. Earl's quotes 7 minutes and is usually done in 3–6.

The Bistro is the opposite. It never showed an estimate on any of our 11 orders, and it's by far the slowest: 10 to 41 minutes, median 30, with 9 of 11 orders taking 21 minutes or more. The one place a student most needs to plan around is the one place the app says nothing.

One more thing the second dataset made visible: when an order is late, it can be very late. 19 of the 110 estimated orders came in after the quoted time, and 8 of those were 8 or more minutes late (one was 27). A single number hides that tail. A range shows it.

What this data can and can't say. It's two students' orders, at the times we happen to eat, and only 11 from the Bistro, so it shows a pattern, not a proof. I also looked for a lunch-rush effect and didn't find a clear one: rush-hour orders took a median of 8 minutes vs. 6 otherwise, and the app was still early during the rush. "Completed" is when staff marked the order ready for pickup, which is the moment that matters for planning. The receipt may also leave out an estimate the app showed on screen.

03 · What changed in the design

Design for the gap, not the hunch

My first idea

Recalculate every estimate around burner capacity, because the timer is too short.

After the data

Most timers are fine, just padded. The fix belongs where there is no timer: give the Bistro a real estimate.

Today

One confident number ("7 min") that's usually wrong by a few minutes.

Redesign

A range from past orders: "usually 3–6 min." Honest about spread, and still easy to read.

Today

At the Bistro, a blank.

Redesign

"Usually 21–33 min." Then, if the student has class soon, a warning before they pay, with an exit: "I'll walk fast, order anyway."

My first warning

"Get a latte from Earl's instead." One fixed suggestion, whether or not it actually helps.

Redesign

Nearby options that would still get you to class, each with its usual wait, how early you'd arrive, and a tap straight into that menu.

Today

Menus are text only. Dish names like "Loco Moco Plate" or "City Slicker" mean nothing if you've never had them.

Redesign

A photo on every dish. This came from my friend, not from me: she said it's the feature she wants most, and that it matters most for international students choosing food they don't know by name.

Missing

The app never learns whether its estimates were right.

Redesign

One tap after pickup: "Was it ready when you got there?" That is the honest version of my receipt study, built into the product with consent.

05 · A security call

Why .ics files instead of WebReg

The warning needs to know when your next class is. My first instinct was to pull a student's schedule straight from WebReg. That's UCSD's own login system, and scraping it is a real security problem. Almost every calendar app can export a standard .ics file, so I designed around that instead. Same result, far less risk, and the student never hands over a password.

06 · Reflection

The data changed the design

If I had built my first idea, I would have "fixed" estimates that mostly weren't broken and missed the one that didn't exist. A friend's 48 orders confirmed the main pattern, so it isn't just my lunch habits. Next: talk to Bistro staff about why there's no estimate, test the warning and the nearby options with students, and bring this to HDH Dining, who run the app. Students use it every day, but only the university can change it, and anonymized order data from them would turn two students' receipts into a real model.
