The 30-Minute Lie

I set out to prove that UCSD's dining app gives wrong wait times. Then I pulled 77 of my own orders, and the data told a more interesting story.

Role
Solo: data collection, analysis, design
Data
77 of my own Triton2Go food orders, May–Nov 2026, from emailed receipts
Tools
Python, Figma
Finding
Where the app gives an estimate, it pads it: 53 of 66 orders were ready early. The Bistro, the slowest spot, never gave an estimate at all: 9 orders, 10 to 41 minutes, median 22.

01 · The hunch

I thought the timer was lying

This started as me being annoyed, not as a design exercise. I'd order at the Bistro before a 1pm lecture and end up standing outside way past when I expected to eat. My theory: the app estimates every dish the same way, active tickets times an average prep time, and ignores that a wok station only has so many burners.

That theory made a nice story. Before designing around it, I wanted to check it.

02 · Testing it

77 receipts

The app blocks screenshots, but every order sends an email receipt with the order time, the app's target time, and when the order was marked complete. I collected 77 of my food orders from May to November 2026 and turned them into a dataset: location, dish, time ordered, time quoted, time actually completed.

The lie wasn't the number. It was the missing one.

At the places that give an estimate, the estimate is padded, not optimistic: 53 of 66 orders (80%) were ready early, typically 4 minutes before the quoted time. Earl's quotes 7 minutes and is usually done in 3–6.

The Bistro is the opposite. It never showed an estimate on any of my 9 orders, and it's by far the slowest: 10 to 41 minutes, median 22, with 7 of 9 orders taking 21 minutes or more. The one place a student most needs to plan around is the one place the app says nothing.

What this data can and can't say. It's one person's orders, at the times I happen to eat, and only 9 from the Bistro, so it shows a pattern, not a proof. "Completed" is when staff marked the order ready, which isn't always when I actually had food in hand. The receipt may also leave out an estimate the app showed on screen.

03 · What changed in the design

Design for the gap, not the hunch

My first idea

Recalculate every estimate around burner capacity, because the timer is too short.

After the data

Most timers are fine, just padded. The fix belongs where there is no timer: give the Bistro a real estimate.

Today

One confident number ("7 min") that's usually wrong by a few minutes.

Redesign

A range from past orders: "usually 3–6 min." Honest about spread, and still easy to read.

Today

At the Bistro, a blank.

Redesign

"Usually 21–34 min at this time." Then, if the student has class soon, a warning before they pay, with an exit: "I'll walk fast, order anyway."

Missing

The app never learns whether its estimates were right.

Redesign

One tap after pickup: "Was it ready when you got there?" That is the honest version of my receipt study, built into the product with consent.

05 · A security call

Why .ics files instead of WebReg

The warning needs to know when your next class is. My first instinct was to pull a student's schedule straight from WebReg. That's UCSD's own login system, and scraping it is a real security problem. Almost every calendar app can export a standard .ics file, so I designed around that instead. Same result, far less risk, and the student never hands over a password.

06 · Reflection

The data changed the design

If I had built my first idea, I would have "fixed" estimates that mostly weren't broken and missed the one that didn't exist. Next, I'd collect receipts from a few other students (with their permission) to see if the Bistro pattern holds beyond my own lunch habits, and test whether a range actually helps people decide faster than a single number.
