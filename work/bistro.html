The Missing Estimate

I set out to test whether UCSD's dining app's wait times were accurate. Then I pulled 125 real orders, mine and my friend Linda Song's, and found the real gap was the estimate the app never shows.

Role
Solo: data collection, analysis, design
Data
125 Triton2Go food orders from two students (77 mine, 48 from Linda Song), May–Nov 2026, from emailed receipts
Tools
Python, Figma, HTML/CSS/JS
Finding
The Bistro never showed an estimate: 11 orders, 10 to 41 minutes, median 30 (a small sample). Elsewhere, estimates were mostly cautious: of 110 orders, 84 were early and 19 (17%) late.
Status
Diagnosing why the Bistro is different before designing more: meeting HDH Dining, who run the app, on Nov 19.

01 · The hunch

I thought the timer was lying

This started as me being annoyed, not as a design exercise. I'd order at the Bistro before a 1pm lecture and end up standing outside way past when I expected to eat. My theory: the app estimates every dish the same way, active tickets times an average prep time, and ignores that a wok station only has so many burners.

That theory made a nice story. Before designing around it, I wanted to check it.

02 · How Triton2Go works today

The flow students use now

Triton2Go is UC San Diego's mobile ordering app for campus dining, run by HDH Dining. A typical order:

  1. Pick a dining location and add items.
  2. At checkout, most locations show a target pickup time. Some, like the Bistro, show nothing.
  3. Pay, then wait for the app's alert that the order is ready.
  4. Walk over and pick it up. An emailed receipt records when you ordered, the target time, and when staff marked it complete.

That receipt became my dataset. Getting it was its own obstacle: the app blocks screenshots (the screen goes white), and receipts only go to the account's own email, so a friend has to forward hers to share.

03 · Testing it

125 receipts

I collected 77 of my food orders from May to November 2026. To check that this wasn't just my habits, Linda forwarded hers too: 48 more. For each order: location, dish, time ordered, time quoted, time completed.

The problem wasn't the number. It was the missing one.

The Bistro never showed an estimate on any of our 11 orders, and it's the slowest spot: 10 to 41 minutes, median 30, with 9 of 11 taking 21 minutes or more. That's only 11 orders from two people, so it's a strong signal, not proof.

Everywhere else, the gap was smaller than I expected. Of the 110 orders that showed an estimate, 84 (76%) were ready early, 7 on time and 19 (17%) late. Eight of those 19 were 8 or more minutes late, and one was 27.

So the gap I'm closing is specific: 15 of 125 orders (12%) came with no estimate at all, 11 of them at the Bistro, plus a smaller tail of badly late orders (8 of 110, about 7%).

What this data can and can't say. It's two students' orders, at the times we happen to eat. I also looked for a lunch-rush effect and didn't find a clear one: rush-hour orders took a median of 8 minutes vs. 6 otherwise, and the app was still early during the rush. "Completed" is when staff marked the order ready for pickup. The receipt may also leave out an estimate the app showed on screen.

04 · What I don't know yet

Why is the Bistro different?

The receipts show that the Bistro is slow and silent, not why. Before designing anything big, I need answers the data can't give me:

  • Who marks an order complete, and when? Food bagged, or placed on the pickup shelf?
  • Why does the Bistro show no estimate? An app setting, made-to-order cooking, or a kitchen choice?
  • Is a 30–40 minute wait normal there? Or are my 11 orders outliers? I'm calling an order "late" when it's completed after the quoted time, and "very late" at 8+ minutes. I don't yet know how HDH defines it.

So I asked. HDH Dining's business analytics manager agreed to meet on Nov 19, and I plan to talk to Bistro staff next. I'll update this section with what they tell me.

07 · Ideas I'm not building yet

Hypotheses, not findings

A few ideas came up that my data doesn't support yet, so I'm keeping them as things to test with students rather than designing them out:

  • A late-for-class warning before paying, using a calendar file (.ics) the student exports, rather than their WebReg login.
  • Nearby spots that would still get you to class on time, when your first choice is slow.
  • Dish photos on the menu. Linda suggested this for international students who don't know dishes by name.

08 · Reflection

The data changed the question

If I had built my first idea, I would have "fixed" estimates that mostly weren't broken and missed the one that didn't exist. The next step is the one I skipped at first: understanding why the Bistro is different, from the people who run it. After that, I'd test the first fix and the ideas above with students before building anything bigger.
