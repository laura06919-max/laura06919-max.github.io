The Missing Estimate

I set out to test whether UCSD's dining app's wait times were accurate. Then I pulled 125 real orders, mine and a friend's. Then I asked the team that runs the app why the data looked the way it did. The answer changed what was worth designing.

Role
Solo: data collection, analysis, stakeholder interview
Data
125 Triton2Go food orders from two students (77 mine, 48 a friend's), May–Nov 2026, from emailed receipts
Tools
Python, Figma
Finding
The app's estimates were mostly cautious, not optimistic: 84 of 110 orders were ready early. The Bistro, the slowest spot, keeps its quoted time off receipts because it holds orders in a queue during rushes. The app's vendor is already rolling out AI-adjusted estimates, expected in 2027.
Outcome
Instead of redesigning something the vendor is already fixing, I'm surveying international students about dish photos, a change HDH Dining controls and has agreed to prioritize if students say it matters.

01 · The hunch

I thought the timer was lying

This started as me being annoyed, not as a design exercise. I'd order at the Bistro before a 1pm lecture and end up standing outside way past when I expected to eat. My theory: the app estimates every dish the same way, active tickets times an average prep time, and ignores that a wok station only has so many burners.

That theory made a nice story. Before designing around it, I wanted to check it.

02 · How Triton2Go works today

The flow students use now

Triton2Go is UC San Diego's mobile ordering app for campus dining, run by HDH Dining. A typical order:

  1. Pick a dining location and add items.
  2. At checkout, the app shows a "Ready in" time, like "Ready in 24 min" at the Bistro.
  3. Pay, then wait for the app's alert that the order is ready.
  4. Walk over and pick it up. An emailed receipt records when you ordered, the target time, and when staff marked it complete. Bistro receipts leave the target time out.

That receipt became my dataset. Getting it was its own obstacle: the in-app receipt screen turns white if you screenshot it, and emailed receipts only go to the account's own email, so a friend has to forward hers to share.

03 · Testing it

125 receipts

I collected 77 of my food orders from May to November 2026. To check that this wasn't just my habits, a friend forwarded hers too: 48 more. For each order: location, dish, time ordered, time quoted, time completed.

The estimate I most needed to check was the one the receipts left out.

The Bistro is the slowest and least predictable spot: 10 to 41 minutes, median 30, with 9 of 11 orders taking 21 minutes or more. That's only 11 orders from two people, so it's a strong signal, not proof.

None of its receipts recorded a target time, and at first I read that as the app showing no estimate. Then I checked the app itself: checkout does show one ("Ready in 24 min" on Nov 17). The receipt is what leaves it out, so there's no record to check it against. If 24 minutes is typical there, Bistro orders usually take longer than quoted, the opposite of everywhere else. One screenshot can't show that, so I now screenshot the Bistro's quote every time I order.

Everywhere else, the gap was smaller than I expected. Of the 110 orders that showed an estimate, 84 (76%) were ready early, 7 on time and 19 (17%) late. Eight of those 19 were 8 or more minutes late, and one was 27.

So the gap is narrower than I first thought: the Bistro's estimate is the one nobody can check, at the spot where waits vary the most, plus a smaller tail of badly late orders elsewhere (8 of 110, about 7%).

What this data can and can't say. It's two students' orders, at the times we happen to eat. I also looked for a lunch-rush effect and didn't find a clear one: rush-hour orders took a median of 8 minutes vs. 6 otherwise, and the app was still early during the rush. "Completed" is when staff marked the order ready for pickup. Bistro receipts don't record the quoted time, so for those 11 orders I know the actual wait but not the estimate.

04 · Asking the people who run it

The receipts couldn't tell me why

My data showed what was happening, not why. So I emailed HDH Dining, who run Triton2Go, and they connected me with their business analytics manager, who manages the system. Here is what I asked and what I learned.

Why don't Bistro receipts record a quoted time?
The Bistro gets around a hundred orders the moment it opens, so it's the only location set to hold orders in a queue and print about 20 tickets at a time. While an order is held, the app says "waiting to release," and the student can still cancel. The quoted time only attaches once the ticket prints. A held order means a wait of at least about 45 minutes.
How is the "Ready in" time calculated?
From two settings HDH controls: how many orders a kitchen can handle per hour, and a prep time for each dish. Extra items on one ticket add a little more time each. The system can't vary prep time by time of day, and the first "Ready in" screen doesn't know when a kitchen is running behind.
Why were most of our orders early?
On purpose. Kitchens are short-staffed at the start of the year, so prep times are padded in fall, then trimmed later in the year as teams settle in.
What counts as late?
HDH's dashboard calls an order on time if it's ready within 3 minutes of the quote, and they aim for most orders to land there. By that definition, 40 of our 110 orders (36%) were on time. Most of the rest were early, which fits the fall padding.

05 · Already being fixed

The estimate isn't mine to redesign

Triton2Go is third-party software, so HDH can only change the settings the vendor exposes. The vendor has already piloted AI-adjusted estimates that react to real-time load and staffing. At one UCSD location, orders ready within 3 minutes of the quote went from about 80% to 98%. A campus-wide rollout, including the Bistro, is expected in 2027.

The most useful thing my data did was tell me where not to design.

06 · What I'd do differently

I prototyped too early

Before I understood the problem, I designed a checkout that showed a wait range at the Bistro and warned students if an order might make them late to class. A UX engineer who reviewed this case study put it plainly: with discovery incomplete, no prototype should have been made yet. He was right. The Bistro's "missing" estimate turned out to be a deliberate queue setting, and the estimates themselves are being rebuilt by the vendor.

I'm keeping the early prototype here as part of the process, not as the answer.

07 · Where this goes next

A problem I can actually move

One thing came up in the conversation that HDH controls directly: dish photos. Most menu items have no picture, which matters for students who don't recognize a dish by name. HDH has wanted photos for years, but had never heard from students that it changes what they order.

So that's my next step: a short survey of international students on how photos would affect what they order. HDH Dining has agreed to share the results with their team to help prioritize photos in the coming year.

08 · Reflection

Discovery first, then design

My first hunch was wrong, and my first design was early. What made this project worth doing was the order I eventually followed: test the assumption with real data, take the data to the people who run the system, and only then decide what to build. Sometimes the answer is that the fix is already on its way, and the most useful work is somewhere else.
